Why Firefox Blocks Extensions in Private Browsing

This article explains the security and privacy reasons behind Mozilla Firefox's decision to disable browser extensions in Private Browsing mode by default. It covers the inherent risks of third-party add-ons, how extensions can intentionally or accidentally record your private browsing activity, and why requiring explicit user permission is necessary to maintain a truly private session.

The Purpose of Private Browsing

Firefox’s Private Browsing mode is designed to erase your digital footprint on your local device. When you use a private window, Firefox does not save your visited pages, search history, cookies, or temporary files. However, this built-in protection only controls what the browser itself does, not what third-party software installed inside the browser might do.

Extensions Have Deep Access to Your Data

For most browser extensions to work correctly, they require significant permissions. Depending on the extension, it may have the ability to:

  • Read and modify all data on the websites you visit.
  • Monitor your network traffic and web requests.
  • Access your browsing tabs and activity.
  • Store data locally on your hard drive or sync it to an external server.

If an extension runs during a Private Browsing session, it retains all of these capabilities, effectively bypassing the privacy barriers Firefox puts in place.

Preventing Accidental Data Leaks

Many extensions are not malicious, but they are poorly coded when it comes to respecting privacy modes. An extension designed to improve productivity, check grammar, or manage tabs might automatically log the URLs you visit or cache website data to function properly.

If allowed to run in Private Browsing by default, these extensions could accidentally write your private activity to your computer's hard drive or sync it to a cloud account. This completely defeats the purpose of opening a private window.

Stopping Malicious Tracking

While Mozilla vets extensions on its official store, some add-ons still engage in data harvesting. Developers may collect your browsing habits to sell to data brokers or advertising networks.

When you open a Private Browsing window, you likely have a higher expectation of anonymity. By blocking extensions by default, Firefox ensures that a rogue or invasive add-on cannot secretly monitor your most sensitive browsing sessions.

Reducing Browser Fingerprinting

Websites can track you without cookies by using a technique called browser fingerprinting. This method identifies you by looking at your browser's unique configuration, including your screen resolution, operating system, and installed extensions.

Many extensions alter the code of the web pages you visit. Websites can detect these alterations to figure out which extensions you are using, making it easier to identify and track you across the web. Disabling extensions in Private Browsing makes your browser look more generic, protecting you from this type of tracking.

Putting the User in Control

Mozilla recognizes that some extensions—such as password managers and ad blockers—are essential even in Private Browsing. Instead of banning them outright, Firefox uses an opt-in system.

When you install an extension, Firefox explicitly asks if you want to allow it to run in Private Windows. This forces you to evaluate how much you trust the developer of that specific extension before granting them access to your private sessions.
