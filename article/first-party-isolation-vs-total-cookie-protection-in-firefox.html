First-Party Isolation vs Total Cookie Protection in Firefox

This article explains the key differences between First-Party Isolation (privacy.firstparty.isolate) and Total Cookie Protection in the Firefox web browser. While both features are designed to stop cross-site tracking by compartmentalizing your browsing data, they differ significantly in how they handle website compatibility. First-Party Isolation takes an absolute, rigid approach that often breaks modern web features, whereas Total Cookie Protection is a modern evolution that dynamically grants temporary exceptions for essential functions like single sign-on services.

What is First-Party Isolation (FPI)?

First-Party Isolation, enabled via the privacy.firstparty.isolate preference in Firefox's about:config menu, is a feature originally developed for the Tor Browser and later brought to Firefox.

FPI works by keying all browser data—including cookies, cache, DOM storage, and TLS session identifiers—to the primary domain shown in the address bar (the first party). If a tracker is embedded on three different websites you visit, FPI forces the browser to create three entirely separate data profiles for that tracker. Because the tracker cannot combine this data, it cannot follow you across the web.

The downside to FPI is its rigidity. It makes zero exceptions for legitimate cross-site communication. Consequently, enabling privacy.firstparty.isolate frequently breaks websites that rely on third-party logins (like "Sign in with Google" or Microsoft SSO), embedded payment gateways, and certain content delivery networks.

What is Total Cookie Protection (TCP)?

Total Cookie Protection (internally known by Mozilla as Dynamic First-Party Isolation, or dFPI) is the direct successor to FPI and is now enabled by default in Firefox.

Like FPI, Total Cookie Protection creates a separate "cookie jar" for every website you visit. Despite having "Cookie" in the name, TCP also isolates other forms of browser storage, such as LocalStorage and the browser cache, preventing trackers from using alternative methods to identify you across different sites.

The defining feature of TCP is that it is dynamic. It understands the difference between invisible tracking and intentional user interaction. If you try to use a third-party login button or an embedded comment system, TCP detects your interaction and makes a temporary, localized exception. It allows the third-party service to access its cookies just enough to log you in, without exposing your browsing history to passive trackers.

The Core Differences

  • Website Compatibility: FPI is strict and breaks many modern websites, particularly federated logins. TCP uses heuristics and the Storage Access API to dynamically allow cross-site data sharing only when necessary for site functionality.
  • Accessibility: FPI is a hidden setting that must be manually toggled via privacy.firstparty.isolate in about:config. TCP is enabled by default in Firefox's standard privacy settings.
  • Development Status: Mozilla actively maintains and updates TCP to ensure it balances privacy and usability. FPI is largely considered a legacy feature within standard Firefox, maintained primarily for downstream projects like the Tor Browser.

Which One Should You Use?

For standard web browsing, you should rely on Total Cookie Protection and leave privacy.firstparty.isolate set to false.

Enabling both at the same time is counterproductive. Because FPI is an older, absolute rule, turning it on overrides the dynamic exceptions of Total Cookie Protection. You do not gain twice the privacy by enabling both; instead, you simply disable the "smart" functionality of TCP and revert your browser to the stricter, site-breaking behavior of FPI.
